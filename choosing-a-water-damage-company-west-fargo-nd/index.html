How to Choose a Water Damage Company in West Fargo, ND

In an emergency it is easy to hire the first company that answers. A few questions will tell you who is ready to do the work properly.

Pick a water damage company by how it answers specific questions, not by how loudly it advertises. The best signs are a valid state license, a written scope, moisture readings and a clear explanation of who is responsible for what. Weak signs are pressure, vague prices and a promise that sounds too good to check.

Questions to ask on the first call

  • Are you licensed as a contractor in North Dakota, and what is the license number?
  • Do you carry liability insurance?
  • Will you provide a written scope of work before work starts?
  • Do your crews follow the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard?
  • How do you measure moisture, and will I get the readings?
  • Who handles the rebuild, and is it priced separately?
  • How do you work with my insurance company?

A real company has these answers ready. If the person on the phone cannot answer, ask for a manager.

Licenses and what the state says

State law requires licensing once a job passes $4,000. Look the contractor up on the Secretary of State's search tool to confirm the license is in good standing, and expect the number to be printed on your contract. Verify it yourself instead of trusting a business card.

Be wary of out-of-state crews who show up after storms. The Attorney General warns that some rent temporary offices to appear local. The Insurance Commissioner adds a warning about unsolicited contractors and public adjusters who promise fast fixes or payouts.

The Attorney General's office suggests comparing several bids, putting the repairs in a detailed written contract and limiting the up-front payment to materials. Hold the final payment back until you are satisfied with the work.

Emergency help versus a bid war

Water damage is time sensitive. You may not have time for three bids on the first day. The practical approach is to authorize emergency mitigation, which is water removal and drying, from a company you have vetted by phone. Rebuild bids can wait until the structure is dry. The water extraction page explains what the first day looks like.

Some companies do mitigation only, and some do rebuild as well. Either is fine as long as the scope tells you which. Be careful of any company that will not put mitigation and rebuild on separate lines.

What to look for in the Fargo-Moorhead area

Cold weather changes how drying works. A company serving this area should be able to explain how it handles a house that is heated low or empty in January, since cold air holds little moisture. Ask whether the crew uses desiccant dehumidifiers or supplemental heat on winter losses.

Distance and availability matter too. West Fargo, Fargo and Casselton all sit along I-94, and a company that dispatches from the metro can usually reach any of them the same day. Ask for an estimated arrival time and what happens if the crew is delayed by weather.

Check the paperwork after the job

  1. Get a copy of the final scope and any change orders.
  2. Keep drying logs and final moisture readings.
  3. Request photos from before, during and after.
  4. Save invoices and receipts for your insurer.
  5. Make sure you have the license number and contact for the company.

If something is wrong later, those records are your proof. They also help if you sell the home.

Mistakes to avoid when hiring

  • Signing a work authorization without reading it, especially one that assigns your insurance benefits.
  • Agreeing to a price over the phone with no written scope.
  • Choosing a company only because it is the cheapest, since low prices may mean less drying.
  • Letting a company start demolition with no photos or readings first.
  • Paying the full amount before the work is done.

An assignment of benefits form can give a company the right to deal directly with your insurer. Some homeowners like that, and others do not. Read it, ask what it means and keep a copy.

If you feel rushed, slow down for five minutes. A good company will respect that, and a pushy one has told you something useful.
