Commercial water damage restoration is the extraction, drying and repair of water losses in offices, shops, restaurants, warehouses and multi-unit buildings. For a business, water means more than wet floors. It means lost sales, damaged inventory and tenants who cannot work. If you find water in your building now, shut off the water if you can do so safely, protect inventory and call the 24/7 line.

Commercial water emergency: first steps

  • Shut off the building's main water valve or the affected branch.
  • Keep staff and customers out of wet areas and away from outlets.
  • Move inventory, files and electronics out of the water if it is safe.
  • Photograph the loss and note the time you found it.
  • Call dispatch, your insurer and your property manager.

If water has reached the electrical panel or a sprinkler system is discharging, call the utility, the fire department or 911 before the restoration line. The first call is for safety.

Why large buildings dry differently

A wide floor with high ceilings means more air to condition, and concrete slabs hold moisture for a long time. Large buildings in West Fargo, such as the arena, recreation center and fairgrounds buildings, show the scale problem. The air movers and dehumidifiers required are of a different scale than a house.

Commercial crews plan equipment by zone, so the part of the building that matters most for your business can be dried first. That often lets a shop or office reopen in stages.

How a commercial restoration job runs

  1. Stop the source and secure the building, including after-hours access for the crew.
  2. Extract water and protect stock and equipment.
  3. Map moisture across the whole structure.
  4. Remove materials that cannot be dried, such as soaked ceiling tile and carpet tile.
  5. Dry in zones with air movers and dehumidifiers.
  6. Document everything for your insurer and your landlord, then rebuild.

Records carry more weight in a commercial claim. Ask your insurer about business interruption coverage, and log closed hours and damaged stock. Related guidance on documenting a loss sits in the water extraction and flood damage pages.

Protecting stock, records and equipment

Inventory and records are often worth more than the floor under them. Lift boxes onto shelves or pallets, move paper files to a dry room and unplug electronics only if the outlet and the floor around it are dry. Do not turn on equipment that was wet until it has been checked.

Make a list as you go, with the item, where it was and roughly what it is worth. An adjuster will ask for it, and it is much easier to write while the loss is fresh than a week later.

Tell your property manager or landlord right away if you rent. A leak that began in another unit or on the roof may be their responsibility, and a quick report helps pin down the source.
