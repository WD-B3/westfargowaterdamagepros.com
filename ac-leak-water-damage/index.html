AC leak water damage is damage from condensation or leaks at an air conditioner, air handler or its drain line. A cooling system pulls moisture out of the air and sends it out through a drain, and when that drain clogs the water has nowhere to go.

If you see water at your unit or a new stain on a ceiling, turn the system off at the thermostat and call the 24/7 line if the material is wet.

Signs of an AC leak

  • A drip or puddle at the indoor unit, in a closet or in the attic.
  • A new brown stain on a ceiling near a vent or air handler.
  • A musty smell that is stronger when the system runs.
  • Water in the overflow pan or float switch tripping the system off.
  • Sweating on ducts or vents in humid weather.

Humid summers and short cooling seasons in the Red River Valley

West Fargo has warm, humid summers followed by a long winter in which the cooling system sits idle. A condensate drain that gathered debris over the cold months can be found plugged on the first humid week of summer, when the system runs hard for the first time.

Check the drain line and pan before the cooling season, and look at the unit after the first few hot days. A small cup of water in the pan is normal. Overflow is not.

How a crew treats AC leak damage

Because the water is usually from condensation, it is generally clean at the start, but it often sits in warm, dark spaces where growth begins quickly. That is why the first job is to find out how far the moisture has traveled.

  1. Turn the system off and stop any dripping.
  2. Scan the area with a moisture meter and thermal camera, including the ceiling and wall cavities near the unit.
  3. Remove soaked insulation and drywall that cannot dry.
  4. Dry with air movers and a dehumidifier, keeping the HVAC off until readings are safe.
  5. Repair and repaint once the area is dry.

Wet attic insulation is the biggest hidden problem. It looks fine from below and stays wet for a long time, so it is checked and replaced if needed. Structural drying explains how the readings guide the work.

Keeping a condensate line from failing again

Most AC leaks trace back to a clogged condensate drain line or a cracked drain pan. Algae and dust build up in the line over a cooling season until the water has nowhere to go but the pan, then the floor.

A technician can flush the line and check the float switch, which shuts the system off when the pan fills. If your air handler sits in an attic, a closet or above a finished ceiling, a small overflow can soak insulation and drywall before anyone notices.

After a leak, ask the technician to confirm the drain is clear before the system is turned back on. Restoration dries what the water reached, and the HVAC company fixes the cause.
