Prepare Your West Fargo Home for Storm Season: Water Checklist

Most storm water damage starts with a small weak point. A weekend checklist finds many of them before the weather does.

Get ready for storm season by checking the places where water gets in: the roof, gutters, windows, foundation grading and sump pump. A few hours of inspection and a few cheap repairs beat a restoration job later. Finish the list before the heavy rain and hail season in late spring and early summer.

Outside the house

  • Clear gutters and downspouts, and extend downspouts several feet from the foundation.
  • Check the roof for missing or lifted shingles and failed flashing around vents and chimneys.
  • Look at caulk and seals around windows and doors.
  • Make sure the soil slopes away from the foundation.
  • Clear window wells of debris and check their covers.
  • Trim limbs that could reach the roof or siding in high wind.

If you see damage, call a roofer or contractor and verify the license before signing anything. The North Dakota Attorney General says a contractor license is required for jobs over $4,000.

Inside the house

Test the sump pump

Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the pump start, move the water and shut off. Check the discharge hose, and make sure it empties outdoors and away from the house. A battery backup is worth considering, since storms often knock out power exactly when the pump is needed most.

Sump problems are a leading cause of basement water. If your lower level has finished space, read basement water damage cleanup to understand how crews pump and dry a wet basement.

Local rules and conditions worth knowing

Under West Fargo rules a sump pump must empty outdoors, and the city has said that noncompliant properties may be fined $100 a month, with hoses directed away from buildings and kept off sidewalks. The city's Public Works department answers questions.

The rule exists because sewer flow spikes during storms and melt. In May 2022 the city reported about 11.1 million gallons a day in the sewer against a normal 2.5 to 3 million.

About 24 inches of precipitation falls in a typical year, with the peak arriving in the weeks you are preparing for. Flat ground drains slowly, so standing water lingers around foundations after a heavy storm.

Document before the storm

Following the commissioner's advice, shoot photos and video of the damage and keep an itemized list that gives the make, model and estimated value. Doing a version of that before a storm gives you a baseline. Walk through every room with your phone, film your belongings and store the video somewhere other than your home.

Also review your policy. Standard policies usually cover wind and hail but not flood, so check if you need separate flood coverage or a sewer and sump backup endorsement.

During and right after a storm

  1. Stay away from windows and keep pets with you.
  2. Do not walk through standing water in a basement if outlets are in it.
  3. After the storm, check the attic and top-floor ceilings first.
  4. Place buckets, move belongings and photograph every leak.
  5. Cover openings if you can do it safely, or call a roofer.
  6. Call your insurer, then call dispatch for drying.

The page on storm water damage cleanup explains what restoration crews do after wind-driven rain or hail. A plain roof leak is covered on the ceiling repair page.

Prepare in town and out of town

Homes in Fargo and Casselton face the same storms and the same flat land. Rural lots outside town may have wells, septic systems and long driveways, so also ask your plumber about those.

A one-page storm kit

  • Flashlight, batteries and a phone charger that does not need the grid.
  • Tarp, plastic sheeting and tape for temporary cover.
  • Buckets, towels and a wet vacuum.
  • Printed copies of your insurance policy and agent's number.
  • A list of your main water shutoff and electrical panel locations.
  • Photos or video of your home and belongings.

Keep the kit in one place and tell everyone in the house where it is. In a storm, minutes matter, and nobody wants to search a dark basement for a tarp.

Do not climb on a wet roof to place a tarp. Call a roofer, and cover indoor belongings instead.

Finally, tell your family what to do if you are not home. A teenager who knows to shut the main valve and call a neighbor can save a house.

Write the main shutoff location and your insurance claim number on a card kept near the kit, so anyone can act quickly.
