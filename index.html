Water damage repair and restoration, from the first call to the last repair

When a pipe lets go in a West Fargo kitchen or a sump pump quits during a spring thaw, the first hours decide how large the repair becomes. Water that stays in drywall, subfloor and carpet pad for more than a couple of days starts to swell framing, lift flooring and feed mold. Water damage restoration is the work that stops that chain: extraction, drying, cleaning and, when needed, rebuilding.

This page covers the whole job in plain terms. If your situation has a clear source, such as a burst supply line, a wet basement or a sewage backup, the page for that source goes deeper. If you are not sure what you are looking at, call the line and describe what you see.

Water mitigation

Water mitigation: stopping the damage from spreading

Water mitigation is the emergency part of restoration. It covers shutting off the source, pulling out standing water, removing material that cannot be dried and setting equipment so the rest of the structure dries in place. Mitigation is what keeps a small loss small, and it is the step insurers ask about first.

Repair comes after mitigation. A crew that starts rebuilding before the readings are right is closing wet material inside the wall. That is why the written scope separates the two phases, and why structural drying gets its own page here.

Insurance claims

Insurance and water damage claims

Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a pipe that bursts or an appliance hose that fails, is often covered by a homeowners policy. Slow leaks and long-term neglect often are not. Flood damage is usually excluded from a standard policy and needs separate flood coverage, and a sewer or sump pump endorsement is not the same thing as flood insurance. Check your policy or ask your insurer.

State guidance for policyholders is simple: tell the insurer promptly, limit further damage and record the loss on photo or video. Proof of loss is due within 60 days of the company's request, and the department says an adjuster is generally assigned within 24 to 72 hours of notice. Moisture logs and a written scope make that paperwork easier.

Local conditions

West Fargo and the water risks that come with it

West Fargo sits in the Red River Valley in Cass County, west of Fargo. The city counted about 38,600 residents in the 2020 Census and grew roughly 49 percent in the decade before it.

That growth means many homes are fairly new, so leaks here more often start at appliance hoses, water heaters and supply lines than at decades-old pipe, and basements and crawl spaces from new subdivisions get their first real tests during a wet spring.

River flooding is the risk most people think of first. The West Fargo Diversion, part of the Sheyenne River diversion system that became operational in the fall of 1992, routes Sheyenne River floodwater past West Fargo and Horace.

Engineers say it has been called on more often than first expected, and the record floods of 1997, 2009, 2010 and 2011 tested it. The diversion does not stop a failed sump pump, a burst pipe or a sewer backup inside your house, so those still call for the pages above.

Winter is the other driver. Long subzero stretches and roughly 51 inches of snow are why frozen pipes and ice-related ceiling leaks belong on any West Fargo homeowner's radar. The city's smart water meters let customers turn on usage alerts, which gives early warning when a hidden leak starts running.
