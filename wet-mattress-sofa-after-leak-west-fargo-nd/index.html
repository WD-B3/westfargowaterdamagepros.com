Wet Mattress or Sofa After a Leak: Save It or Toss It?

After a leak, the hardest question is often what to keep. The water type and the time it sat decide the answer more than the price tag.

A wet mattress, sofa or rug can sometimes be saved if the water was clean and drying begins quickly. If the water was contaminated, or if the item stayed wet for days, it is usually safer to replace it. The decision rests on three things: what kind of water it was, how long the item was wet and what the item is made of.

Step one: identify the water

Water sourceCategoryWhat it means for belongings
Supply line, water heater, rain through a roofClean water, but can turn worse with timeSoft goods can often be dried or cleaned if handled within a day or so
Dishwasher or washer overflowGray waterCleaning needed, many soft goods are at risk
Toilet overflow, sewer backup, flood waterContaminatedPorous items are usually discarded

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard groups water into categories, and contaminated water is handled very differently. When in doubt, treat it as contaminated and call for help.

Mattresses and bedding

Foam and inner-spring mattresses soak up water and dry slowly in the center. A mattress that got a brief splash of clean water can sometimes be dried on its edge with air movement and sunlight. A soaked mattress, or one that touched contaminated water, is normally discarded. Bedding and clothing that can be washed hot are usually fine if the water was clean.

Sofas, chairs and upholstered furniture

  • Frames made of solid wood can often be dried and saved.
  • Particleboard frames swell and fail.
  • Foam cushions and batting hold water and mildew quickly.
  • Leather and fabric may stain or shrink as they dry.
  • Furniture that sat in sewage water is almost always discarded.

Lift furniture out of standing water right away. Put foil or plastic under the legs, and keep wet cushions standing on edge so air reaches both sides.

Rugs, books, papers and electronics

Wool and synthetic area rugs can often be cleaned and dried by a professional. Books and papers can be frozen to stop damage until they can be dried. Electronics that were wet should not be turned on, and a technician should check them before use. See carpet water damage restoration for wall-to-wall carpet and pad.

Photograph before you toss

The commissioner recommends an itemized list of damaged belongings along with photos and video of each affected area. Do that before you carry anything to the curb. Your insurer may want to see a damaged item, so ask before you throw out something large and expensive.

  1. Photograph each item where it sits.
  2. Write the item, age and approximate value on a list.
  3. Keep a small tag, label or serial number if possible.
  4. Ask your insurer before disposal of valuable items.
  5. Dispose of contaminated items safely.

Winter belongings and basement storage in the Fargo area

Many homes in the region keep seasonal items, photos and keepsakes in basements. Storage boxes on a concrete floor are the first things to get wet when a sump pump fails. Keep boxes on shelves off the floor, and store irreplaceable papers in sealed plastic bins on an upper level.

Winter adds a second risk. A pipe that bursts while the house is quiet can soak furniture for hours. Moving a sofa or mattress off an exterior wall during a cold snap is a small habit that costs nothing.

Contents cleaning and pack-out

Restoration companies can often move belongings to a dry space, clean them and return them. Ask whether the company handles contents or only the structure, and what the cost is. When the building has to be torn out and dried, moving belongings out first protects them and gives the crew room to work. See structural drying for what happens to the building meanwhile.

Pets, children and cleanup safety

Keep children and pets away from wet materials until they are cleaned or removed. Wear gloves and boots around contaminated water, and wash hands afterward. For a sewage or flood loss, read sewage backup cleanup before touching anything.

A simple triage you can do in ten minutes

  1. Lift everything off the floor and out of standing water.
  2. Sort items into three piles: wash or clean, dry and wait, and discard.
  3. Put clean, washable fabrics through a hot wash if the water was clean.
  4. Set hard items, such as wood furniture, in a dry space with airflow.
  5. Photograph the discard pile before it leaves the house.

Do not rush to throw out everything, and do not hold on to everything either. Items that sat in clean water briefly are often fine, and items that sat for days are the ones to let go.

Write down the date and time you lifted each item out of the water. Elapsed time is one of the main things a restoration pro will ask about.
